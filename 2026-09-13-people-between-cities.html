The People Between Cities: Who Made the Silk Roads Work?

Merchants from Sogdiana connected China to Central Asia without ruling the lands between them. Their letters, tombs, and lawsuits show what such a network could do—and whose protection it could not guarantee.

3,404 words · about 15 min

In the early fourth century, a merchant named Nanai-vandak wrote from western China to his partners in Samarkand. He began with news of other Sogdians: one in Jiuquan was safe, another in Guzang was safe. Of those who had gone farther east he knew almost nothing. No one had come back from the interior for three years. He had heard that Luoyang, the Chinese capital, had been sacked. Then, with the same urgency, he turned to linen prices, a deposit at home, an orphan who depended on its income, and thirty-two packets of musk awaiting delivery. The letter was found in 1907 in the remains of a watchtower west of Dunhuang. It never reached Samarkand, or at least it was still there when the tower was abandoned. Whether it was lost or confiscated is unknown. Nicholas Sims-Williams's translation and Daniel Waugh's introduction; Sims-Williams on the letter and its dating.

That one piece of paper holds a world the familiar phrase Silk Road can obscure. The people involved were not making a single journey from China to Rome. They lived in different cities, sent news and instructions across years of delay, kept money and family obligations in more than one place, and sometimes lost contact altogether. Yet for centuries they moved goods, languages, and practices across a distance no Sogdian government controlled. How did they make that possible? And how much of the work was really theirs?

A home made of separate cities

Sogdiana lay around Samarkand, Bukhara, Panjikent, and other settlements in what is now Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. Its people spoke an Eastern Iranian language. They farmed and made things as well as traded; their cities had rulers, fortifications, and elaborate houses, but they never formed a durable empire spanning the routes on which their merchants worked. A Sogdian abroad therefore could not count on a Sogdian army or magistrate at every stop. Smithsonian's account of Sogdiana; Waugh's introduction to the Ancient Letters.

The geography made a direct crossing costly. East of Samarkand lay high mountains and, farther on, the Taklamakan Desert. Travelers used towns fed by rivers and mountain runoff along its edges. At Turfan, water made farming possible; grapes and other produce grew there. Dunhuang stood near the place where northern and southern routes around the desert converged. Beyond it, the Hexi Corridor led toward the Chinese interior. A town on this route was more than a place to sleep. Residents knew which route was passable, who held local authority, what a commodity would fetch, and which traveler could be trusted. A merchant arriving from another town needed that knowledge more than a map. Smithsonian, historical trade routes.

Over time Sogdians established communities at a succession of such places. Some residents continued east or west; others stayed, farmed, made goods, kept households, or dealt with travelers who did not know the local language. A scholarly study of the seventh- and eighth-century settlements in East Turkestan found both long-distance travelers and merchants whose range was much smaller, supported by resident Sogdians who acted as intermediaries. This is a more plausible way to move a shipment across Eurasia than imagining one caravan, one leader, and one unbroken road. It also explains why the disappearance of news mattered so much to Nanai-vandak: a route depends on people in its towns remaining reachable. Jonathan Skaff, “The Sogdian Trade Diaspora in East Turkestan”.

Trade diaspora

A dispersed population whose settled communities help travelers from the same or related communities trade across regions. It supplies contacts, local knowledge, credit, and translation. Shared origin can make cooperation easier, but it does not replace the law or politics of the places where its members live.

Calling this a diaspora does not mean every Sogdian owed every other Sogdian help. It describes a set of opportunities and obligations, unevenly distributed. A partner in Samarkand might hold your deposit. A resident of Turfan might know a buyer. A priest in Dunhuang might provide charity. Later we will meet someone for whom the same arrangement offered much less.

What the papers actually count

The oldest letters are unusually intimate evidence, but they come from a crisis in the 310s, not from the network's later height. Nanai-vandak reports the destruction of Luoyang in 311 as news that has reached him from afar. He lists men in scattered cities and asks his partners to manage assets in Samarkand. His letter is simultaneously a market report, a family instruction, and an admission that he no longer knows what has happened to many of his associates. Another merchant, writing from Guzang, says there is no departing caravan and inventories pepper, silver, and other goods he cannot easily move. The details are not a generalized picture of normal trade; they show which links mattered when they failed. The Ancient Letters, nos. 2 and 5.

Three centuries later, a different accident preserved a different kind of record. Residents of Turfan made burial garments from used paper. Fragments of accounts, contracts, and official documents survived in tombs because they had been discarded for that purpose. One incomplete customs register from the Gaochang kingdom, before the Tang conquest of Turfan in 640, records thirty-five commercial transactions; a Sogdian was involved in twenty-nine, and in thirteen both parties were Sogdians. That is striking evidence of their prominence in this surviving register. It is not a measurement of their share of all trade across Asia. Étienne de la Vaissière, “Sogdian Trade”; de la Vaissière, “Eastern Iranian Migrations to China”.

The same buried papers upset the image of a population composed entirely of adventurous merchants. Census material names hundreds of Sogdians in the Turfan area, including farmers and craftspeople. Their settled lives were not a footnote to the caravan business. A traveler can return to a market because people remain there: maintaining houses, producing food, marrying, learning local rules, and raising the next generation. The network's mobile members are easier to romanticize than its resident ones, though the latter made repeated movement practicable. De la Vaissière on the Turfan documents.

There was no single commodity or traffic level that defined the route. The letters mention silver, cloth, pepper, and musk; later documents include horses, metal, medicines, perfumes, and enslaved people. Silk appears as merchandise, gift, tax payment, and a means of payment. Its fame has given the roads their modern name, but the evidence comes in fragments, and the total volume of Sogdian trade is unknown. An object found far from where it was made proves a connection; it rarely tells us whether it passed as a sale, diplomatic gift, payment, or loot. De la Vaissière, “Sogdian Trade”; Smithsonian on objects reaching Japan.

The Ancient Letters were found together in a watchtower; the Turfan business records survived because paper was reused in burials. Both are exceptional deposits. They can establish that a practice existed and sometimes show how it worked. They cannot give us a reliable count of all caravans, all Sogdian livelihoods, or the total value of transcontinental trade.

A language that traveled farther than its rulers

Goods were only part of what the Sogdians carried. Their language became widely useful along inland routes. In China, some Sogdians translated Buddhist texts; in the Turkic world, Sogdian was used for administration and writing. The Bugut inscription in Mongolia, one of the earliest known inscriptions of the Turkic empire, is in Sogdian. The point is not that Turks became Sogdian or that every buyer needed to learn the language. A ruler or merchant could employ people who already knew several worlds well enough to turn an order, contract, or sacred text into something intelligible elsewhere. De la Vaissière on Sogdian in the Turkic empire; Smithsonian on the Sogdian language.

The Sogdians were not neutral couriers between sealed civilizations. Their own lives changed in the places they linked. In China, a foreign community could have a sabao, an official who represented it to local authorities. The title had roots in the world of caravan leadership, but its Chinese role joined community affairs to dealings with the state. Scholars disagree about how directly a sabao supervised commerce, and it is not clear whether communities selected these figures before official confirmation or the government appointed them. Those uncertainties matter: the institution was neither simply an independent guild nor merely an imperial office. Smithsonian on the sabao and the debate over the office.

Sabao

A leader of a Sogdian or other Central Asian community in China who mediated with local authorities and had political and religious responsibilities. Its connection to caravan leadership is clear; the precise extent of its commercial authority is disputed.

Wirkak, called Shi Jun in Chinese records, makes the overlap visible. He died in 579. His sons commissioned a house-shaped stone sarcophagus in the region of today's Xi'an, with an epitaph in both Sogdian and Chinese. He had served as sabao; the carved scenes show a family invested in both its standing in China and its ancestral religious world. On one wall, Wirkak and his wife Wiyusi cross the Chinvat Bridge of Zoroastrian belief toward paradise. The object itself is Chinese in form, and its Sogdian story is legible in its names and images. A person could become locally established without leaving the older world behind. The carvings also present how an elite family wanted to be remembered, not an unfiltered record of ordinary Sogdian life. Smithsonian, Shi Jun's sarcophagus.

Here is a useful limit to the idea that connection makes everything circulate. Sogdians helped transmit Buddhism and Manichaeism and translated texts among several languages. Their ancestral Mazdean practices, however, remained especially visible in their own communities and funerary art; they did not spread in China on the scale Buddhism did. The same routes carried different ideas with very different results. Translation required people, institutions, patrons, and an audience, not simply an open path. Smithsonian on Sogdian religious life and translation.

The letter from Dunhuang

There were other letters in the watchtower mailbag. Miwnay, a Sogdian woman in Dunhuang, wrote to her mother and to her husband Nanai-dhat. She said her husband had left her there for three years without money or replies. She asked local men to help her return to her mother. One referred her to a relative; the relative passed responsibility to another man. She borrowed nothing because no one would lend to her. A priest helped her with charity. Her daughter Shayn added that debts left by an associate had forced them into service in a Chinese household. Miwnay's account is one person's testimony, and parts of the letters are damaged, but the repeated requests and refusals are there in her own words. Sims-Williams's translations, letters 1 and 3; Smithsonian's account.

The first response is to call this a failure of the network. It is also a way to understand what the network was. Merchants could distribute risk by leaving capital with partners in Samarkand, using agents in Chinese cities, and receiving help from compatriots at intermediate stops. Miwnay had moved away from the relatives who would ordinarily have supported her. To return, she needed money, a traveling companion, and the consent or help of men able to provide them. The mechanism that made distant trade possible did not guarantee equal mobility to the merchant's family. His freedom to move and her inability to leave were related facts.

Nor is it quite right to say the community gave her nothing. The priest's charity and her appeal to local leaders show that institutions of assistance existed. Their help was too little, or too dependent on other people's cooperation, to get her home. The difference between access to a community and an enforceable claim on it is the important one. A trader's reputation might call forth credit because others expected future dealings with him. Miwnay asked for help without that bargaining position. Her letters tell us what happened in one crisis, not how all Sogdian women lived; they make it impossible, however, to describe the diaspora only as a technology of trust.

Selective protection

A network may reliably support people who control capital, information, or future business while giving weaker claims to dependents and outsiders. Its reach is measured not only by where its members can go, but by who can demand help when a relationship breaks down.

This is a general feature of dispersed communities, though the Sogdian case gives it unusually direct evidence. Distance can give a person options if others at the next destination will help him. The same distance can make another person dependent on those options being granted. Miwnay's two letters crossed neither Asia nor the centuries as intended; their failure to arrive is why we can read them at all.

When trust ran out

Move forward again, to 670. A Sogdian named Cao Rokhshan brought a case before a Tang court in Turfan. He said a Chinese merchant, Li Shaojin, had borrowed 275 bolts of silk from his elder brother and never repaid them. The brother had disappeared on a journey. Li denied both the debt and the partnership. Two Sogdian merchants testified that they had witnessed the loan. No written contract survived. The court accepted their oral testimony and ordered repayment with interest. The surviving documents end before we learn whether Li actually paid. This is one case, not proof that Tang courts were always fair to foreigners. It does establish that a Sogdian could bring such a dispute, and that witnesses from his community could matter under the host empire's law. Valerie Hansen, “Silk Road Cities and Their Co-existing Legal Traditions,” pp. 26–27.

Mixed currency

In Tang frontier markets, bolts of cloth, grain, and coins could all serve as recognized means of payment. The 275 bolts in the Turfan case were capital that could be lent, not merely fabric carried for sale.

Notice how far this is from a story in which an ethnic network simply substitutes for the state. Sogdian witnesses retained the memory of a transaction; Tang judges made that memory consequential to a Chinese debtor. The court could also have refused to hear them, or ruled differently. The merchants had to work within political orders they did not control. In Nanai-vandak's time those orders had been shaken by famine and war. In Rokhshan's, Tang expansion into the oases gave a foreign trader a legal forum. The same empire that could restrict movement could also enforce a claim.

The emperor's silk

There is a larger correction. The picture of independent caravans bringing China's silk to eager buyers assumes that the state stood behind private trade as a friendly guard. Tang rulers were much more controlling. In a recent reappraisal, historian Shao-yun Yang points to laws requiring authorization for foreign merchants to enter and restricting subjects' private travel abroad. Certain Sogdian merchants were permitted to trade across northern and northwestern frontiers if they paid taxes and tolls. Even these licensed merchants were forbidden to export silk from Tang territory under rules repeatedly issued in the eighth century. Smuggling probably occurred; a prohibition is not a count of what actually crossed the border. But a surviving prohibition cannot be squared with the idea that the empire simply invited free private silk export. Yang, “Tang ‘Cosmopolitanism’: Towards a Critical and Holistic Approach”.

At the same time, the Tang state itself moved large quantities of cloth westward to support soldiers and officials. Hansen cites a record of 15,000 bolts sent in 745 to one Dunhuang garrison—far more than the 275 bolts in Rokhshan's lawsuit. Étienne de la Vaissière argues that such frontier expenditure made a market from which Sogdian intermediaries benefited. This does not mean private trade was negligible, nor can those two quantities be added into a total. They describe different flows: an individual loan and a military payment. They show why asking only what merchants carried misses what supplied frontier markets with purchasing power. Hansen on the Dunhuang payment; de la Vaissière on Tang fiscal transfers.

Nor should every beautiful foreign-looking object in Chang'an be filed under voluntary exchange. Yang argues that a significant part of the Tang capital's celebrated diversity, including some performers, came through enslavement and forced tribute. Sogdian merchants themselves traded enslaved people, as lists of commodities and surviving contracts show. That history does not erase the work of translators or the reality of cultural curiosity. It makes the phrase cosmopolitan city insufficient if it describes only what elites enjoyed, not how the people who supplied it arrived. Yang on the human costs of Tang elite culture; de la Vaissière on traded commodities.

The strongest account is neither a road operated by merchants alone nor a system designed by one empire. Resident communities connected distant markets; local rulers and courts made some transactions possible; imperial spending supplied goods and demand; coercion also moved people. The surviving records support these mechanisms. They do not tell us their relative contribution to all Eurasian trade, and the balance changed between the fourth and eighth centuries.

What remained when the arrangement changed

The conjunction did not last unchanged. Arab conquest altered Sogdiana in the early eighth century. The rebellion begun by the Tang general An Lushan in 755, and the wars that followed, altered Tang control of the western regions and its frontier finances. Scholarship describes a decline in the Sogdians' earlier role as overland intermediaries while maritime connections and other merchants became more important. That is a change in a particular commercial arrangement, not proof that exchange across Asia stopped or that every Sogdian community vanished at once. Oxford Research Encyclopedia, “The Sogdian Merchant Network”; de la Vaissière on the later trade.

One family's names show a slower change than any political date. In excavated Chinese tombs, the surname Shi—associated with Sogdian Kesh—continued for at least seven generations, while Sogdian given names appear in the first four generations of sons. That pattern suggests both persistence and assimilation. It does not tell us what each descendant thought of himself. It does show that a commercial diaspora could become a population with a history in its new home, not just a series of visitors awaiting a return caravan. Smithsonian, the nine Sogdian surnames.

The Sogdians made the Silk Roads work in the precise sense that people living between major powers supplied repeated contacts, languages, local knowledge, and continuity. But a route is not made by its travelers alone. Its courts, soldiers, tax collectors, households, priests, and coerced laborers belong to the account too. We would have to revise the relative weight given here to merchants and states if more complete records showed that private long-distance trade dwarfed frontier spending, or if legal records showed that foreigners rarely obtained enforceable judgments. The available documents point in a different direction: the network was real, useful, and dependent on conditions outside itself.

Nanai-vandak could write instructions for an orphan in Samarkand amid disaster in western China. Miwnay, in the same set of surviving letters, could not get from Dunhuang back to her mother. Neither letter arrived where its writer wanted. The distance between those two kinds of reach is part of the history of the road.
