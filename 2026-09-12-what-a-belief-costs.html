What a Belief Costs: Is the Mind Logical, and What Is It For?

For fifty years the case that the mind is not logical has been a list of its failures. Four cards, a hollow mask and a table of four numbers show what the tests measured instead. The mind reasons carefully wherever a false belief costs its holder, and most beliefs are never billed.

4,840 words · about 21 min

Four cards lie on a table. They show E, K, 4 and 7. Each has a letter on one side and a number on the other. The rule to be tested says that if a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other. The task is to name the cards, and only the cards, that must be turned over to find out whether the rule is true.

Peter Wason set this task at University College London in 1966. The answer logic requires is E and 7. A vowel with an odd number behind it breaks the rule, so E must be turned; an odd number with a vowel behind it breaks the rule, so 7 must be turned; the 4 cannot break the rule whatever is on its back, and neither can the K. In Wason's own runs fewer than one subject in ten gave E and 7. Most gave E alone, or E and 4. The task was repeated in many laboratories over the next twenty years with the same result, and it became the first item in the case that ordinary people cannot handle a single conditional sentence. The case grew into the catalogue everyone now knows: anchoring, the overweighting of vivid outcomes, the fast machine and the lazy overseer. Is the mind logical? The catalogue says no.

1. The wrong measuring stick

The catalogue's first item has been examined twice since 1966, and it did not survive either examination intact.

In 1982 Richard Griggs and James Cox, at the University of Florida, kept the structure and changed the content. The rule became a law: if a person is drinking beer, they must be over nineteen, which was Florida's drinking age at the time. The cards read beer, coke, 22 and 16. Turning beer and 16 is the same choice as turning E and 7. About three quarters of subjects made it. Nothing in the logic had changed.

Seven years later Leda Cosmides showed that the improvement was not familiarity. It appeared for any rule with the form of a social contract, a benefit taken and a price owed, including invented rules attributed to invented tribes, and it vanished for rules just as familiar that had no such form. Her explanation, that the mind carries machinery specialised for catching cheats, is still contested. The pattern is not.

People still fail the abstract version, though. The second examination asked whether failing it is a failure at all. In 1994 Mike Oaksford and Nick Chater changed not the cards but the question put to the subject. Suppose the person is not doing a logic exercise but trying to learn whether a rule holds in the world, at a cost of one card per look. Which card is most worth turning? The answer depends on how common the things named in the rule are, and Oaksford and Chater assumed they are rare, because the things rules mention almost always are: most cards are not vowels, and most numbers are not the one even number the rule names. Under that assumption the expected information from each card comes out in the order E, 4, 7, K. That is the order in which subjects choose the cards, and close to the proportions in which they choose them. The E-and-4 answer that fails Wason's test is the best answer to a different question, and the different question is the one people meet outside a laboratory.

They called the method rational analysis. Before scoring a behaviour as an error, state the problem the person is actually solving, with its goal, its costs and the world it is met in; work out the best solution to that problem; then compare. Gerd Gigerenzer's group applied the same procedure to the famous base-rate problems, restated the probabilities as counts of people, and watched most of the errors disappear. Not all of them. People still anchor on irrelevant numbers and still overweight the vivid case, and no restatement of the problem has removed those.

Rational analysis

Before calling a behaviour an error, state the problem the mind is actually solving, with its goals, costs and environment; find the best solution to that problem; then compare. Many catalogued biases are good solutions to the real problem scored against the wrong one. Named by John Anderson (1990); applied to the four-card task by Oaksford and Chater (1994); Griggs and Cox (1982) and Cosmides (1989) supplied the content effects; Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) the frequency results.

What the two examinations revealed is narrower than a rehabilitation and more useful. The catalogue's first item measured the mind against formal logic on abstract material. That is a recent, trained skill, like reading, and the failures on it are failures of the skill. They say nothing about whether the mind solves the problems it was built for, because the item never asked what those problems are. To find out, go down a level, to the eye.

2. Seeing is already believing

Take a face mask off a wall, the kind with a nose and brow moulded in, and turn it so that you are looking into the hollow back of it. Light it from one side. You will see a face, nose towards you, as if the mask were the right way round. You can know that it is hollow, hold it in your hands, run a finger down the inside of the nose, and still the eye reports a face coming out at you. Turn it slowly and the face turns the wrong way. Nobody has learned to switch this off.

What the eye is doing here is what it always does. The signal that leaves the retina is flat, inverted, blurred at the edges and missing a patch where the optic nerve enters. What you see is not that signal. It is the brain's best guess at what in the world would have produced it, and the guess is made from what the world has usually contained. Faces are always convex, so a shading pattern that could be either is read as convex, and the reading wins over the knowledge. Hermann von Helmholtz described perception this way in 1867 and called it unconscious inference. The idea was unfashionable for a century. It is now the standard account, under the name predictive processing.

The wiring supports it more strongly than most people expect. The signal from the eye passes through a relay in the thalamus before reaching the visual cortex, and that relay receives far more fibres running down from the cortex than up from the eye. The brain is not mainly listening to the eye. It is mainly telling the eye what to expect, and what travels upward is largely the difference between the expectation and the signal.

One group of people can see the mask as it is. Patients with schizophrenia are fooled by it far less often than healthy subjects. The usual explanation is that in these patients the expectation carries less weight against the senses, which is also one account of why they hear voices that are not there. Trust the signal over the expectation and you will read noise as a voice.

So belief does not begin where perception ends. The mind commits to a guess about the world before any signal reaches the parts of it that can talk, weighting expectation against evidence by how reliable each has been. The same heavy expectation that lets you recognise a friend in bad light at forty metres makes you see a face in a hollow mask, a pattern in a run of coin tosses, and a confirmation in an ambiguous result. Not two systems, one intuitive and one rational. One operation, at different distances from the retina.

Unconscious inference

Helmholtz (1867), now called predictive processing: what you see is the brain's best hypothesis about what caused the signal, prior expectation and sensory evidence combined, each weighted by its reliability. More fibres run down to the sensory relays than up from the senses; what travels upward is largely the error. The hollow-mask illusion and its weakness in schizophrenia (Schneider and colleagues, 2002; Dima and colleagues, 2009) are the standard demonstrations.

3. The family it belongs to

Now go up a level. What kind of thing is a machine that predicts from expectation and corrects from data?

Any predictor that learns from a sample errs in two ways. Bias is the error of a model too simple to capture the true pattern. Variance is the error of a model flexible enough to fit the noise in the sample it saw, which then predicts new cases badly. When observations are few, variance dominates, and a simple rule that ignores most of the information predicts new cases better than a rich model that uses all of it. Herbert Simon called the mind's simplicity a compromise forced by limited time. It is not a compromise. Under limited data it is the more accurate strategy, and that is a theorem, the same one behind the shrinkage in the earlier reading on chosen options.

The test was run on rules of the kind the mind uses. Take twenty real datasets, on anything from the populations of German cities to the prices of used cars, and set two methods against each other. One is multiple regression, which weighs every available clue. The other is a rule called take-the-best, which finds the single most reliable clue, decides on it, and ignores the rest. Fitted to data they had already seen, regression won. Asked to predict cases they had not seen, the one-clue rule was slightly more accurate. It threw away most of the information and predicted better for it.

The same structure was built on purpose, and then flown. In 1960 Rudolf Kálmán published a method for tracking a moving object from noisy measurements: keep a prediction from a model of the motion, take each new reading, and correct the prediction by an amount that depends on how uncertain it is compared with the reading. NASA adopted it, and the filter navigated the Apollo spacecraft to the moon. It is, term for term, Helmholtz's account of perception. The operation that makes a hollow mask look convex is the operation that landed on the Sea of Tranquility.

Bias-variance trade-off

A predictor learning from limited data errs by bias, from being too simple, and by variance, from fitting the noise in its sample. With few observations variance dominates, so a rule that ignores information predicts better than one that uses it all. The mind's heuristics, statistical shrinkage and the Kálmán filter (1960) are one trade-off in three fields. The twenty-dataset test is Czerlinski, Gigerenzer and Goldstein (1999); Simon's "bounded rationality" is from 1955.

Given the mind's real problems, its real data, and what computing actually costs, we have so far found it about as rational as it could be. If that were the whole story the advice would be short: set the machine to the right problems, in the right notation, and trust it. I do not think it is the whole story. The machine fails, and not where the catalogue looks.

4. What reason is for

A subject sits at a screen and answers a reasoning problem, then writes out the argument for her answer. Some time later she is shown a set of arguments, supposedly from other participants, and asked to judge whether each one is any good. One of them is her own, written minutes earlier. About half the subjects in this experiment, run in 2016, did not recognise it. And of those who did not, more than half rejected their own argument as unconvincing, tending to reject the weak ones and keep the strong, which is what a good judge does. The same mind, on the same argument, was lax as its author and strict as its critic.

That is a strange thing for a truth-finding organ to do, and the standard account says that is what reasoning is: a slow, careful checker that catches the fast machine's mistakes. If so, it is badly built for the job. It is lazy, which every side admits. And it has a side: when people reason about something they already believe, they look for reasons that support it and stop when they find some. A checker with a thumb on the scale is a poor design.

Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber proposed in 2011 that reason was never built for solitary truth-finding. It was built for arguing: to produce justifications that persuade other people, and to evaluate the justifications other people offer. On that account the bias is the specification. An advocate makes the best case for their side; the other side's case is the other side's job. The evaluating half should be demanding, because a mind that accepted every argument it heard would be exploited by every liar it met. The subject who rejected her own argument was running the evaluator on the producer's output, and the two halves are not meant to meet in one head.

They are meant to meet across a table. Give Wason's abstract task to a person alone and about one in ten solves it. Give it to groups of five or six and let them argue, as an experiment did in 1998, and three groups in four solve it, including groups in which nobody had the right answer at the start. Reason works in dialogue among people who want the right answer. Run alone, it rationalises.

Argumentative theory of reason

Mercier and Sperber (2011): reasoning evolved to produce arguments that persuade others and to evaluate the arguments others offer, not to find truth alone. It predicts a lax, one-sided producer and a demanding evaluator, which is what experiments find: the own-argument study is Trouche, Johansson, Hall and Mercier (2016); the group result is Moshman and Geil (1998).

5. The turn: the same organ in reverse

A table of four numbers. Of 298 patients who used a new skin cream, 223 got better and 75 got worse. Of 128 who did not use it, 107 got better and 21 got worse. Did the cream help? The big numbers say yes, 223 against 107. The proportions say no: three quarters of the users improved, and five sixths of the people who never touched the cream improved without it. The cream did harm. You have to compare fractions to see it, and most people compare the big numbers instead. People who are comfortable with fractions get it right more often. So far this is a test of arithmetic, and it behaves like one.

Now change the labels and nothing else. The rows are no longer patients but cities, some of which banned carrying concealed handguns and some of which did not. The columns are no longer better and worse but crime went down and crime went up. The four numbers stay where they are. The arithmetic is the same arithmetic. Dan Kahan and three colleagues put both versions to more than a thousand Americans, having first measured each person's comfort with numbers and their politics, and this is what they found.

Picture one subject: comfortable with fractions, a gun owner, opposed to the ban. Her table shows the ban followed by less crime. She looks at it, and she does not compare the fractions. She compares the big numbers, or finds some other route, and reports that crime went up. Beside her, someone equally good with numbers who favours the ban reads the same table and gets it right. Give the two of them the opposite table, where the ban is followed by more crime, and they swap: now he gets it wrong and she gets it right. Among people who are poor at arithmetic, the two sides are only a little apart. Among the best, they are far apart. Skill did not close the gap. Skill is what opened it.

This is not one study's oddity. The best-informed partisans are the best at finding flaws in arguments against their side and at forgiving flaws in arguments for it, and of all the reasoning errors that intelligence protects against, this is the one it does not. Section 4 said reasoning works when the people arguing want the right answer. Here they did not want it, and the better reasoners did worse.

The point I want to insist on is that our gun owner is not being stupid. She is doing a sum, and the sum is correct. Her belief about the ban changes nothing about the ban; her vote is one in a hundred million, and no crime in her city will rise or fall because of what she thinks. Her belief does change how she stands with her brother, her church, the men she shoots with. Hold the wrong belief there and the cost is friendships, trust and standing, and the bill comes at once. So she has a belief that costs her nothing if it is wrong about the world and a great deal if it is wrong for her people, and she does what anyone does with such a belief: she keeps it, and she uses her considerable arithmetic to keep it. The better her arithmetic, the safer the belief. Kahan calls this identity-protective cognition; the economist Bryan Caplan had called it rational irrationality. It has the shape of the winner's curse from the earlier reading: each person chooses well, and the sum of the choices is a group that has stopped noticing the world, with nobody inside it who has any reason to look.

So the first half of this reading was true and incomplete. The mind is rational about what a belief costs its holder. It is rational about what the belief says only where those two coincide, where being wrong about the world is the thing that costs. That is the guiding principle, and it is not truth and it is not logic. It is the price of being wrong, paid by the believer, in whatever the believer actually pays in.

Identity-protective cognition

Kahan's term for reasoning whose job is to keep the reasoner's beliefs aligned with the group whose standing they depend on. A belief on a public question changes nothing about the outcome and much about one's standing, so the tribal belief is the cheaper one, and reasoning ability is spent defending it. Predicts, correctly, that polarisation grows with numeracy. The skin-cream study is Kahan, Peters, Dawson and Slovic (circulated 2013, published 2017; 1,111 subjects); the partisan-sophistication result is Taber and Lodge (2006); the intelligence result is Stanovich and West; "rational irrationality" is Caplan (2007).

The mind is accurate wherever error is expensive to it, and expressive wherever error is free.

Reasoning ability serves whichever of the two is in charge.

6. Where the structure should appear, and does not

A rule of this shape makes a prediction: change the price and the belief should change. Put a partisan at a screen and ask a factual question on which the parties give different answers. How did unemployment move under the current president? How many American soldiers died in Iraq? Democrats and Republicans answer differently, as everyone expects. Now put a dollar on the table for each correct answer. In an experiment of exactly this design, run in 2015, the gap between the parties fell by about half. Add a smaller payment for the honest answer "I don't know", and it fell by about four fifths. The answer given for free was a cheer. The answer given for a dollar was a claim.

Weather forecasters are the other side of the same test. When a forecaster for the United States National Weather Service says seventy per cent chance of rain, it rains about seventy per cent of the time; a study of their forecasts in 1977 found them almost perfectly calibrated. The forecaster gets the bill tomorrow, in public, on every forecast. The political experts Philip Tetlock followed for twenty years barely beat chance, and their bill arrives late, ambiguous and addressed to no one. Same brains, different prices.

The third case changes what the word belief means. A parent believes in a god who heals, and when the child falls ill takes the child to the hospital, and feels no contradiction. The philosopher Neil Van Leeuwen argued in 2014 that this is not hypocrisy: religious credence is a different kind of state from factual belief. It does not respond to evidence, it operates in some settings and not others, and its job is to mark who one is. Dan Sperber had drawn the same line in 1997 between intuitive beliefs, which the mind forms itself and acts on, and reflective beliefs, held on the authority of a source, often half understood, and not wired to action the same way. This is why a belief that vaccines are dangerous coexists with vaccination. Two different kinds of thing are held, and only one of them is priced.

Reflective belief

Sperber (1997): a belief held on the authority of a source rather than formed by one's own perception or inference. Reflective beliefs may be half understood, are insulated from the evidence that would test them, and do not drive action the way intuitive beliefs do. Van Leeuwen (2014) made the same case for religious credence. The paid-partisan experiment is Bullock, Gerber, Hill and Huber (2015); the forecaster calibration is Murphy and Winkler (1977); the experts are Tetlock (2005).

So "most people believe X" is two sentences. The kind of belief that steers a hand is priced and, within the limits of the evidence, accurate. The kind that is asserted is worn, and costs what a badge costs. Most of what appears in surveys, in arguments and in the catalogue of irrationality is the second kind.

7. Why people believe what they believe

Your doctor writes a prescription and you take it. You believe the drug will help. You could not produce the evidence: you have not read the trials, could not judge them if you had, and do not know the names of the people who ran them. Almost everything you believe is like this. That the earth is four and a half billion years old, that Australia exists, that the bridge will hold. In 1985 the philosopher John Hardwig pointed out that this is true of experts too. He cited a physics paper with ninety-nine authors, no one of whom could vouch for the whole, and argued that rational belief in a modern society is mostly rational trust. Why people believe something is almost always why they trust the source.

The mind is well equipped for that question. It tracks a source's record, its competence, its interest in deceiving, and its agreement with what one already holds, and it discounts accordingly. The evidence that this machinery works is the evidence that mass persuasion fails. Pool forty-nine field experiments on campaign contact in general elections, as two political scientists did in 2018, and the average persuasive effect is zero. The hardest case is propaganda, and the Nazi radio network has been measured. After 1933 the broadcasts increased denunciations and antisemitic acts in districts with a long history of antisemitism, and reduced them in districts without one. Where the ground was prepared the radio licensed the belief; where it was not, it gave offence. People are not gullible. They are loyal. They believe their trusted sources and resist everyone else, including those telling the truth.

This closes the loop. Whom do people trust? Those they depend on and belong to. So beliefs track trust, trust tracks belonging, and belonging is the thing that is priced. For every question a person cannot check and need not act on, the group's answer costs nothing to adopt and something to refuse, and the evidence gets a vote only when being wrong will hurt. Most people believe what they believe because someone they had reason to trust told them, and because nothing they do depends on it being true. That is what all of us do about the thousands of questions we cannot test and never have to act on.

Epistemic dependence

Hardwig (1985): nearly all of anyone's knowledge, a scientist's included, rests on trust in others who could not themselves vouch for the whole. Rational belief is mostly rational trust, and the machinery that allocates trust, Sperber and colleagues' epistemic vigilance (2010), explains why persuasion fails and why beliefs follow belonging. The campaign result is Kalla and Broockman (2018); the radio study is Adena, Enikolopov, Petrova, Santarosa and Zhuravskaya (2015).

The rule predicts that where a person's welfare turns on a contested belief, they will get it right. Some do not. Patients refuse effective treatment on grounds of faith or identity, and die. Either the cost of leaving the community really exceeds the cost of death, in which case the rule holds and the price was higher than an outsider can see; or the pricing model is incomplete. The evidence does not yet decide, and the first reading is uncomfortably able to explain anything.

8. What it is, then

So, is it logical? It has logic where logic pays, and had it long before Aristotle wrote the rules down: in obligations, in counts, in any case where the answer comes back and is charged to the believer. Logic on abstract material in abstract notation is a recent, trained skill, like reading, and the failures that fill the catalogue are mostly failures of that skill.

The most accurate picture I can offer is not a logician with bugs, and not an elephant with a rider either. It is a prediction machine with a price list. Near the senses, and wherever the bill comes fast and comes to the believer, it is as accurate as the data allow; the same arithmetic navigated Apollo. Far from the senses, where nothing the believer does depends on the answer and asserting it buys standing, its beliefs are chosen the way clothes are, and reasoning is the tailor. That is why most people believe what they believe. Someone they had reason to trust told them, and they will never have to pay for being wrong.

I would give this up if any of four measured things had come out the other way: if paying for accuracy did not narrow partisan gaps; if the numerate were less polarised rather than more; if calibration did not follow feedback; if propaganda converted rather than licensed. Each has been measured. What I cannot settle is whether the identity mechanism deserves the word rational, as Kahan says, or whether rational analysis has been stretched to excuse a failure, as Gigerenzer's critics say of the heuristics. The mechanism is the same on either reading, and the word is doing less work than the price is.

A belief that costs nothing to hold wrong is not held. It is worn.

Questions to sit with

  1. Recall: Oaksford and Chater showed that the usual "wrong" answer to Wason's task is right on a different reading of the problem. State that reading in one sentence, and the assumption about the world it depends on.
  2. Connect: The hollow mask, the take-the-best rule and the Kálmán filter are the same operation. Say what the operation is, and what it weighs against what.
  3. Apply: Choose one belief you hold about a public question. What would it cost you to be wrong about it? What would it cost you, among the people you depend on, to change it? If the first is near zero and the second is not, say what that implies about how the belief was chosen.
  4. Judge: If identity-protective cognition is rational for the individual, telling people the facts cannot fix it; only changing the price can. Propose one way to change the price on a contested question without coercion, and say who would resist it and why.
