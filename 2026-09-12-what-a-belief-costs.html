How the Mind Decides What Is Real

Why doesn't experience reliably correct us? The answer depends on how a mind infers the world, chooses what to encounter, and decides which surprises belong to the same story.

3,201 words · about 14 min

In 2005, Petter Johansson and his colleagues asked people to choose the more attractive of two photographed faces. On some trials, a sleight of hand let the experimenter return the photograph the participant had rejected. People often failed to notice. Asked to explain their preference, they could supply reasons for a choice they had not made. The researchers called this choice blindness. Their experiment did not establish that all reasons are inventions, or that intentions are unreal. It established something narrower and more unsettling: a sincere explanation can fail to identify what actually produced a decision. Johansson and colleagues, 2005.

Someone who gives such an explanation need not be defending a political identity. Nothing requires a conflict between truth and self-interest. The person may simply lack access to the process that produced the choice, while having enough information to construct a plausible account of it.

This makes the familiar question—whether the human mind is logical—look incomplete. Logic can test whether a conclusion follows from premises. It cannot guarantee that we have correctly identified our premises, where they came from, or the problem we were solving. A faultless argument can begin with a mistaken account of ourselves.

The harder question is why experience does not reliably correct these mistakes. We are capable of remarkable learning. We can also spend years becoming more certain of something false. What determines which happens?

1. What the senses leave out

Imagine hearing a short, sharp noise in another room. A glass might have broken. Something might have fallen without breaking. The sound alone does not uniquely identify its cause. You bring other knowledge: who is home, what was on the table, whether the window is open. Even before you investigate, some explanations are more plausible than others.

The difficulty is structural. Events produce sensory effects, but the effects usually leave several possible events consistent with them. Seeing and hearing require working backward through that relationship. This is an inverse problem: infer what happened from what it left available to observation.

Inverse problem

Inferring hidden causes from observed effects. Several causes may fit the same observation, so additional constraints or evidence are needed. A clear signal need not have a unique explanation.

The same mathematical difficulty appears in reconstructing an underground structure from seismic waves or an astronomical object from the light that reaches a telescope. These are comparisons between problems, not claims that geologists, telescopes and brains use identical machinery. In each case, observation underdetermines its source.

A useful way to model perception is therefore to ask what different possible worlds would make us observe. This is a generative model: a model of how causes produce evidence. Inference compares those possibilities with what arrives. The important advance is that perception need not reproduce a complete world. It needs to distinguish the possibilities that matter for the task.

In a 2002 experiment, Marc Ernst and Martin Banks studied how people combined sight and touch when judging an object's height. They measured the uncertainty of each sense separately, then predicted how a statistically efficient observer would combine them. People's combined judgments closely resembled those predictions. Vision's influence depended on its reliability relative to touch; it was not simply the permanent senior partner. Ernst and Banks, 2002.

This is strong evidence for a particular computation under particular conditions. It does not establish that the entire brain is an optimal statistician. Nor does behaving like a mathematical estimator mean consciously performing its arithmetic. A scientific model of what a system accomplishes and an account of how its physical parts accomplish it are different achievements.

Generative model

A representation of how possible causes would produce observations. It lets a learner compare explanations, predict new evidence and ask what an intervention would reveal. Probabilistic models of cognition combine such structure with uncertainty; they are a research framework, not an established complete theory of the mind. Tenenbaum and colleagues, 2011.

A mistaken perception is not always a departure from this process. Sometimes it is the result of applying a generally useful expectation to an unusual case. But this possibility is not a universal pardon for error. To explain a mistake as a sensible inference, we must specify the expectation independently, show why the person would have it, and predict when changing the evidence should change the mistake.

Otherwise, saying “the brain must have expected that” merely renames the observation.

2. A true belief and a good decision are different things

Suppose you hear that noise and assign a small probability to a dangerous intrusion. You may check the room even if you think an ordinary accident is much more likely. There is no contradiction. The most probable explanation and the best action need not correspond, because the consequences of overlooking different explanations are unequal.

This distinction matters whenever someone infers a belief from behaviour. A person who buys insurance need not think a disaster is likely. A person who avoids a meeting need not feel certain it will go badly. An action depends on expectations, preferences and available alternatives. One observed choice rarely identifies all three.

It also limits the claim that minds primarily seek truth. Accurate expectations are useful to action, but acquiring them has costs. Before crossing a familiar room, you do not need to identify every object. Before accepting an unfamiliar contract, additional investigation may be worth days. The relevant question is how much better the next unit of thought is likely to make the decision.

Falk Lieder and Thomas Griffiths developed this approach under the name resource-rational analysis. Instead of comparing a person with an ideal thinker who has unlimited time and memory, it compares procedures under computational constraints. A shortcut can produce less accurate answers while being preferable once the cost of obtaining an answer is included. This is a proposal for explaining patterns of cognition, not a demonstration that every human shortcut is the best available one. Lieder and Griffiths, 2020.

Resource rationality

Evaluating a thinking procedure by both the quality of its decisions and the time or computation it consumes. A resource-rational explanation needs specified constraints and testable alternatives; “thinking is costly” alone explains very little.

There is a temptation here to turn every failure into a hidden success. If somebody makes a costly mistake, we can invent a still greater cost they supposedly avoided: effort, embarrassment, estrangement. Enough freedom in choosing invisible goals will make almost any behaviour appear optimal. Dobromir Rahnev's critique of resource-rational analysis makes the essential distinction: using optimality as a modelling method does not establish that humans actually behave optimally. Rahnev, 2020.

This is why I would not give the mind a single guiding principle such as “minimise surprise” or “protect social standing.” Such principles can illuminate particular mechanisms. Without constraints on what they predict, they are too accommodating to settle the question.

For the inquiry here, a more useful starting point is a learner that must infer enough about an uncertain world to act within limited time. We can ask where that arrangement fails without assuming that every failure secretly serves it.

3. The experience you never get

Consider a deliberately simplified choice between two machines. One reliably pays four tokens. The other pays either zero or twelve, with equal probability. We know those probabilities; the learner does not. Its long-run average is six, but an early trial can pay nothing.

Now give the learner a familiar rule: repeat what has worked; avoid what has disappointed. After an unlucky first experience, the second machine may be abandoned. The four-token machine continues to supply reassuring evidence. Nothing new is learned about the rejected machine.

A fortunate first experience has a different future. It encourages more trials, during which the initially inflated estimate can be corrected. Pessimism can stop its own correction; optimism often keeps the correction process running. The asymmetry comes from the sampling rule, even if the learner records every encountered outcome honestly.

Jerker Denrell and James March formalised this problem in their 2001 paper on the hot stove effect. Processes that reproduce successful actions can become biased against risky or unfamiliar alternatives that happen to disappoint early. Their analysis also exposes the cost of the remedy: persisting with uncertain options preserves access to good opportunities, but prolongs exposure to genuinely poor ones. Denrell and March, 2001.

Endogenous sampling

The learner's beliefs and actions help determine which evidence becomes available. A conclusion can be distorted because the sample was selected by earlier conclusions, even without dishonest reasoning about the observations received.

This mechanism can operate with money at stake. In a 2010 study, Detlef Fetchenhauer and David Dunning had participants judge whether people shown in short videos would reciprocate trust in an economic game. Participants initially estimated that about 52 percent would reciprocate; the actual proportion was about 80 percent. Financial incentives for accurate judgments did not remove the underestimation. Feedback about everyone—including people participants chose not to trust—brought their final overall estimate to about 79 percent. Feedback restricted to people they trusted left it near 63 percent. Fetchenhauer and Dunning, 2010.

The figures describe this experimental population, not humanity's true rate of trustworthiness. The result matters because the information intervention and the incentive intervention had different effects. Wanting to be right and having access to what would make you right are separate conditions.

Suppose a manager believes a colleague cannot handle difficult work. The manager assigns only routine tasks and sees no evidence of exceptional ability. That is a hypothetical application of the same structure, not proof that the colleague is secretly capable. The unanswered question is whether the colleague would succeed if given the opportunity. Years of routine assignments do not answer it.

We often ask how much experience supports a judgment. We also need to ask what the judgment has prevented the person from experiencing.

4. Why a surprise can leave the old belief intact

Opening access to missing evidence seems to complete the remedy. But it leaves out another decision the mind must make: what is this evidence evidence about?

Imagine a restaurant where you have had several excellent dinners. One meal is terrible. You could lower your opinion of the restaurant. You could instead infer that the regular chef was absent. Both accounts accommodate the observation; they imply different expectations about your next visit. A succession of bad meals might eventually persuade you that the kitchen has changed permanently.

Learning is not just changing a number attached to a fixed category. It can involve deciding whether the category still describes what is happening. This creates a surprising possibility: receiving contradictory evidence, remembering it accurately, and preserving the earlier expectation anyway.

In animal conditioning, repeatedly presenting a previously threatening cue without the aversive outcome can reduce the conditioned response. Yet responding may return after a change of context. Samuel Gershman, Kenneth Norman and Yael Niv describe a latent-cause account: the learner may assign the safe episodes to a different hidden state, retaining what it learned about the original situation. This is one computational explanation of the evidence, not a literal report of an animal's conscious thoughts. Gershman, Norman and Niv, 2015.

Latent-cause inference

Inferring which unobserved situation or process produced an experience. Evidence assigned to an existing cause can revise it; evidence assigned to a new cause can be learned separately. Updating expectations requires deciding what belongs together.

The distinction suggested an unusually revealing experiment. In 2013, Gershman and colleagues compared ways of extinguishing conditioned fear in rats. Instead of abruptly ending tone–shock pairings, one group experienced a gradual reduction in their frequency. The prediction was that a gentler change might be assigned to the existing situation, allowing its learned expectation to weaken. An abrupt change might encourage a separate memory while preserving the original one.

The gradual procedure produced less return of fear in tests after a delay or after unsignalled shocks. A comparison group received the same number of additional shocks in a different sequence, helping distinguish the schedule from the mere presence of extra shocks. This supports the state-discovery account, although behaviour alone cannot prove that the original memory was erased. Gershman and colleagues, 2013.

That is a deeper complication than “people ignore inconvenient facts.” A learner can take the facts seriously and still decide that they do not apply to its old model. The more dramatically the new experience differs, the easier it may be to classify it as another situation entirely.

Applying this mechanism to adult convictions is an inference, not a result established by the rat experiments. It suggests asking whether counterevidence was rejected, or accepted but classified as an exception. Those processes require different explanations and potentially different remedies.

Nor is making exceptions inherently unreasonable. The chef really can be absent. A changed workplace really can be safer than the old one. A learner that forced every observation into one undifferentiated average would lose the ability to recognise regimes and contexts. The difficulty is choosing when to revise a model and when to distinguish another case. More evidence cannot settle that choice without assumptions about which differences matter.

5. Other people are part of the explanation

So far, the learner has mostly encountered things directly. This is already unrealistic for much of what we call knowledge. You cannot personally reconstruct all the measurements behind an account of the solar system, check every component in an aircraft, or reproduce the scholarship behind a history of a language.

John Hardwig's 1985 essay on epistemic dependence examined how a person can have reason to believe something through another person's expertise without possessing the underlying evidence themselves. The dependence extends into science: contributing specialised knowledge does not mean independently mastering every contribution on which a conclusion rests. Hardwig, 1985.

Epistemic dependence

Relying on other people's knowledge for beliefs one cannot fully verify independently. The epistemic question becomes partly whether that reliance is justified: who checked the claim, with what competence, and through which chain of dependence?

This changes the appropriate unit of analysis. A person's inability to explain a result may indicate ignorance. It may instead indicate a sensible division of intellectual labour. Demanding that everyone believe only what they can derive unaided would destroy much of the knowledge the demand is meant to protect.

But dependence changes what counts as corroboration. If five people each inspect a bridge, their agreement can carry information from five inspections. If all five repeat one inspector's report, it carries one inspection through five mouths. Hearing five confident statements does not distinguish these arrangements.

This does not mean that independence is always preferable to discussion. In 2011, Jan Lorenz and colleagues found that social information could bring estimates closer together without a corresponding improvement in collective accuracy, while increasing confidence. But experiments reported in 2017 by Joshua Becker, Devon Brackbill and Damon Centola found that exchange in decentralised networks could improve group estimates even as opinions converged. Centralised influence made group error more likely to increase. These are bounded findings about estimation tasks, not verdicts on every deliberative institution. Lorenz and colleagues, 2011; Becker and colleagues, 2017.

The contrast matters. “People are conformist” does not tell us whether learning from them will help. We need to know how information and influence are distributed. A network can carry discoveries from people who know something we do not. It can also repeatedly return our own assumptions to us with additional apparent witnesses.

The question about why people believe what they believe therefore cannot end at personality, intelligence or loyalty. It includes the history of the evidence available to them and the people through whom that evidence arrived.

6. What would actually change your mind?

A single belief can survive through several quite different routes. You may never encounter the relevant observation. You may encounter it but misunderstand it. You may understand it and assign it to an exceptional case. You may distrust its source. Or you may accept its truth while finding that it changes none of the choices available to you.

Calling all of these “bias” loses the distinctions needed to investigate them. Calling all of them “rational” loses the possibility that something is wrong.

My best working picture is a mind that constructs explanations under uncertainty, acts through those explanations, and learns from the evidence its actions and relationships make available. This is a synthesis of mechanisms with different bodies of evidence, not a claim that one experimentally established algorithm explains perception, political conviction, memory and consciousness. It answers the question about belief better than an unrestricted slogan because its parts can be tested separately.

If avoidance preserves an error, providing outcomes for rejected options should help even when incentives stay fixed. If evidence is learned as a separate situation, changing how experiences are grouped should affect whether learning transfers. If agreement is mistaken for independent corroboration, revealing common sources should reduce its force. Repeated failures of these predictions, in tasks where the proposed conditions genuinely hold, would count against using this account there. A story that labels every outcome compatible with its mechanism has ceased to explain.

This also makes self-knowledge less comfortable. The opening experiment showed that asking ourselves why we chose something does not always recover its cause. By the same token, a fluent answer to “what would change my mind?” is not yet evidence that the proposed event would do so. One can sincerely describe a willingness to revise while continuing to choose experiences that make revision unlikely.

A better question is concrete: what could I arrange to observe that would distinguish my explanation from its strongest alternative? For the hypothetical manager, it might be a bounded assignment that permits the colleague's ability to become visible. For the restaurant, it might be finding out whether the kitchen actually changed. For agreement among experts, it might be tracing which observations their assessments share.

None guarantees a reversal. The colleague may struggle; the restaurant may have declined; independent experts may still agree. The point of a useful test is that the world gets to determine which answer survives. Until there is such a test, having more reasons for a belief may mean that we understand it better, or only that we have become better at explaining why we already hold it.

Questions to sit with

  1. When can avoiding a bad experience also prevent you from discovering that your judgment was wrong?
  2. Why might an unexpected result create an exception instead of changing an existing belief? When would that be the correct response?
  3. Choose one important belief. What observation would distinguish it from its strongest alternative—and do your current habits allow that observation to reach you?
